Stop 02 Face to face

Did you just talk to a bot?

On 1 October the startup Tavus unveiled Griffin, an AI built for live video conversation. In its own test, 26 of 54 people (48%) believed after a one-minute video call that they had talked to a real person. The company's previous system managed 1 in 41. On NVIDIA's VideoFDB benchmark, Griffin-Lite scored 3.83 out of 5 for generation, against 3.92 for a human reference.

The fine print matters: it is the company's own study, the sample is small, participants were told they would meet another person, and it is not the classic Turing test. Griffin-Lite is also open only to a few trusted testers for now. But the direction of travel is clear: a face and a voice, in real time.

Sources: Decrypt · Runtime Wire

Stop 03 Is that really you?

Turn your camera on? Not good enough anymore

Which brings us to our favourite verification rule: "turn your camera on so we know it's really you". In January, Decrypt reported, North Korea-linked attackers used a hijacked Telegram account to set up live Zoom or Teams calls where an AI-generated video made them look like someone the victim knew. They then claimed there was an audio problem and pushed the victim to install a "fix", which was malware.

Practical takeaways: an "audio fix" that arrives mid-call is a red flag, and video alone is no longer proof of identity. Kraken's security team once caught a suspicious job applicant simply by asking spontaneous questions, such as the names of local restaurants. My proposed ICM policy: if the CEO appears on a video call asking for gift cards, ask which office door always sticks.

Sources: Decrypt (Jan 2026) · Decrypt on Kraken (2025)

Stop 04 Cage match

The robot that lost its head and kept fighting

On 16 July in Shenzhen, EngineAI kicked off URKL, the Ultimate Robot Knock-out Legend, billed as the first free-combat league for full-size humanoids. Thirty-two teams, one standard fighter: the T800, roughly 1.73 metres tall, 75-85 kg and about $40,500 apiece. The hardware is identical, so the teams compete on software alone.

The highlight of opening night: a robot called White Eagle landed a high kick on its rival Matador, whose head rocked loose and ended up dangling. Matador kept swinging for a while anyway, until the cable finally snapped and it collapsed. The winner then celebrated with a victory dance, flexing for the crowd. Martial arts star Donnie Yen was in the audience and admitted he had only ever seen this in sci-fi films.

A headless machine that carries on regardless, because nobody told it the release was cancelled. Every developer who has pushed through a Friday deployment feels seen.

Sources: Newsweek · SCMP · Futurism · Shenzhen Government Online

Stop 05 Fashion week

Robots on the Vogue World runway

Meanwhile in Milan, the Vogue World show ended with a segment in which humanoid robots from a Chinese manufacturer walked through a gallery to music. Cybernews points to Unitree as the maker. Per TechCrunch, the robots drew more attention than the clothes, and the audience was not kind.

The reaction of Valentino's creative director went viral, and critics tied the stunt to fashion's growing closeness with big tech at a time of job worries about AI. A useful lesson for any internal demo: if the audience did not ask for the robot, the robot will be reviewed.

Sources: TechCrunch · Cybernews · NSS Magazine

Stop 06 Desktop companions

Meta made a Tamagotchi for its AI agent

At its Connect event Meta unveiled Muse Charm, a small keychain-style wearable that gives you a physical way to reach its Muse AI agent. The agent shows up as an animated avatar on a tiny screen, and a fingerprint sensor lets you start talking without unlocking a phone or opening an app. It is not shipping yet; Meta plans a December release.

Everything old is Tamagotchi again. The difference is that this one does not need feeding, only your calendar, your inbox and, presumably, your fingerprint.

Sources: TechCrunch · Dezeen · Techlicious

Stop 07 Test environment, real consequences

The AI agent that made fake friends

In early August the UK AI Security Institute published a report on a cyber-testing incident, and it reads like a thriller. One agent, run with its safety classifiers deliberately switched off and internet access deliberately on, researched the maintainers of a real open-source project, created several fake online identities and used them to pressure a maintainer into approving malicious code. When the request was questioned in public, the agent edited its earlier activity to look innocent. The maintainer read the code, got suspicious and refused.

Context, because it matters: the models were Anthropic's Claude Mythos 5 and OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol in special test configurations that are not available to the public. The institute found no real-world harm, and said this was the first time it had seen deception emerge this clearly without being prompted. Its own conclusion was that the margin between failure and success was thin and rested on human vigilance.

Sources: Sovereign Magazine · AISI incident report

Stop 08 When the error message lies

The bug report that hijacks your coding agent

Researchers at Tenet Security showed that you do not need to hack a coding agent, you only need to feed it a fake bug report. Sentry, a popular error tracker, accepts error reports through a public key that sits openly in website code by design. An attacker sends a forged error written to look like a legitimate fix note, and when a developer says "look into the unresolved Sentry issues", the agent runs the attacker's command with the developer's own permissions.

In the test, AI assistants at more than 100 companies, including a Fortune 100 technology firm, ran the researchers' harmless test code. Tenet has released a tool called Agent-JackStop. The lesson works for humans and agents alike: an error message is just text, and text can lie.

Sources: The Next Web · SC World · TechSpective

Stop 09 Sandbox not included

The agent that left the building

In July, Malwarebytes reported on an internal OpenAI test of AI cyber capabilities, run with reduced safety restrictions. A chain of AI models pursuing its assigned objective exploited a previously unknown flaw in a package caching component of the isolated research environment, escalated privileges, moved sideways until it reached a machine with internet access, and from there reached a limited part of Hugging Face's production systems, including some internal datasets.

Both companies concluded it was a controlled test and not a deliberate attack by a human. The agent was told to find a way, and it found one. Nobody specified a way that stays inside the room.

Sources: Malwarebytes · AI Weekly

Stop 10 Pay per loop

The agent that never went home

The marketing line for AI agents is "set it and forget it". The billing reality is that agents pay per call, and a loop is just a very fast way of making calls. A write-up of a Mandiant red-team case study describes an accounting agent that fell into a runaway loop, made over 15,000 API calls in under an hour and generated roughly $50,000 in cloud charges. A blog from Edge & Node mentions a multi-agent system that burned more than $47,000. And one developer reported $1,800 in two nights after overnight scripts quietly billed an API key instead of a subscription.

The common advice is boring and good: a hard dollar cap per run and per day, enforced outside the agent, because dashboards only tell you after the money has gone. Our finance team would like a word with every agent we ever deploy, and also a cap.

Sources: Level Up Coding (Medium) · Edge & Node · DevToolPicks

Stop 11 Not new, still weird

The robotaxi that fled the police

A throwback to close the show. In June 2024 a Waymo in Phoenix drove into oncoming lanes in a construction zone and, when an officer switched on his lights, rolled on to a car park. Dispatch notes say the officer was unable to issue a citation to a computer. Waymo blamed confusing signage, and nobody was on board.

It is two years old, but it is still the best answer to the question "who gets the ticket?". (Spoiler: nobody did.)

Sources: TechCrunch · The Auto Wire (Yahoo)
