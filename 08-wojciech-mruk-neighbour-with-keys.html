The Neighbour With the Keys, or: AI “Is Taking Our Jobs” Again ☕☘️

From a suspicious neighbour in Ballyfermot to protein folding and burnt Roman scrolls: why the newcomer is usually less scary than the headline.

7 stops. One mug. Seven minutes, give or take a chat.

Stop 01 Pour the coffee

Kettle on, and a deep breath

Morning, all. Grab a proper mug of strong black coffee. Today we're putting the databases, tables and deployments to one side and talking about something bigger: why everyone keeps telling us AI is coming for our jobs, and why history is doing its favourite thing again.

Let's be honest. Most articles online make it sound as if AI is about to take our jobs, take over the world and, on top of that, ruin the Irish weather. (Fair play, the competition there is fierce.) The fear goes like this: They are arriving, newer, faster, cheaper, and soon they'll have our desks, our chairs and our access to the office coffee machine.

But hold on a second. We've seen this film before, and in Ireland we saw it fairly recently.

Stop 02 The confession

“They’re taking our jobs”

Remember when all those people from Eastern Europe arrived here years ago? They landed, settled in, and yes, quietly took a share of the work. I have a confession to make: I'm one of them. I turned up, found a desk, started writing code and pretended to understand what the locals were saying after the third pint.

Just when we thought the market had settled, along came another newcomer. This one isn't even a person. It doesn't drink Barry's tea, it never complains about the rain, and it can chew through a million lines of code in five seconds. Here they come again, taking our jobs!

Do we pack our bags? Not necessarily. New technology is like new people: at first we keep a polite distance, because nobody likes strangers on the home patch.

Stop 03 A story

The neighbour from Ballyfermot

My old neighbour in Ballyfermot, Dublin, was a case in point. If you saw him on the street you wouldn't hand him your life savings. Shady, a bit suspicious, with the look of a man whose CV has some interesting chapters.

Then came the day I slammed my front door and found myself outside in the rain in a hoodie. And what do you know: his CV included a deep "locksmithing" chapter. Before I'd even thought about breaking a window, he took out a bit of wire, worked the lock with the patience of a watchmaker, and had the door open in four seconds.

Two doors down lived another neighbour who, for reasons known to nobody, had spare keys to most of the houses on the street. He wandered around the estate in his slippers, humming something about keys and secrets.

The moral from Ballyfermot is simple: what looks dangerous, odd or suspicious can, in the right hands and at the right moment, be exactly what gets you out of trouble. And AI is much the same.

Stop 04 Déjà vu

History keeps doing this

Here's a fun one. The word "computer" used to be a job title. Rooms full of people, many of them women, did calculations by hand, including at NASA. When the electronic machines arrived, those people didn't vanish. Some of them, like Dorothy Vaughan, taught themselves FORTRAN and ended up running the new machines. The job didn't disappear. It changed its shirt.

When spreadsheets turned up in the late 1970s, people worried that accountants and bookkeepers were finished. What happened was more spreadsheets, more analysis, and a lot more questions for the accountants to answer. Nobody ever missed doing long division on paper.

None of this means every change is painless. It means the pattern is usually "the work moves", not "the work ends". And it's worth knowing who gets to hold the new keys.

Stop 05 Picking locks

The neighbour who opened biology

Before we panic that AI will take our last SQL procedure, let's look at where it did things human brains simply couldn't do in time.

For about fifty years scientists worked on the "protein folding problem". To understand a disease and design a drug you need to know the shape of the protein involved, and working out the structure of a single protein could take months or even years of painstaking lab work.

Then came AlphaFold from DeepMind. In 2022 it was used to predict the structures of over 200 million proteins, covering nearly every protein known to science, and put them in a database anyone can search. In 2024 its creators shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. It helps researchers with work on diseases and new medicines. It doesn't hand out cures, but it opened a door science had been rattling since before many of us were born.

Stop 06 Prying eyes, kindly meant

Scans, surgeons and scorched scrolls

In medicine, algorithms can look at X-rays, mammograms and MRI scans and flag small changes that are easy to miss on a long shift. A human still makes the call. Think of it as a second pair of eyes that never needs a coffee break. In the operating theatre, robot-assisted systems controlled by surgeons give a steadiness that doesn't wobble after a hard night on call.

In history, when Vesuvius erupted almost 2,000 years ago, it buried a library in Herculaneum and turned its scrolls into lumps of charcoal. Unroll one and it crumbles. In 2023 and 2024 the Vesuvius Challenge showed that machine learning, working on 3D X-ray scans, could pick out faint traces of ink inside the rolled scrolls, and the first passages of text were read without ever opening them. We opened a door to ancient history without picking up a hammer.

Stop 07 Last sip

So what does this mean for us?

It's easy to picture AI as a giant Terminator about to pull the plug on our server. Take away the drama and you get something much more useful: a very good helper. Like our humming neighbour, it has access to patterns and data we'd never have the time or patience to dig through by hand.

We don't have to trust it blindly. We don't have to hand over every key or sign over the car. (See the last post for how to keep the spare keys safe.) But just as Ireland absorbed programmers from Poland and India and came out of it rather well, we can absorb AI into our daily work:

  • Let it take the "jobs" none of us enjoy: writing tedious tests, hunting typos in stored procedures for eight hours, repetitive code.
  • Keep what no algorithm can replace: strategic thinking, architecture, solving real business problems, and having coffee together on a Friday morning.

So the next time someone says "new people have arrived and they're taking our jobs", smile and think of the neighbour from Ballyfermot. Sometimes the least expected arrival is the only one who can open the lock we've been standing in front of, in the rain.

Over to you. Who was your Ballyfermot neighbour, the one who looked suspicious and then saved the day? Tell me at the coffee machine.

Stay curious, keep your spare keys safe, and have a grand Friday. ☕
